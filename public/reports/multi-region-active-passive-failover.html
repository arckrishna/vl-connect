1. Introduction

Context of multi-region active-passive failover and FailoverLab virtual environment

Cloud applications may serve users from more than one geographic region to improve availability. In an active-passive design, one region handles normal traffic while another region remains ready to take over. If the primary region fails, the system must detect the failure, decide whether failover is safe, promote the standby and redirect traffic.

FailoverLab is a browser-based virtual lab for learning how a Multi-Region Active-Passive Failover Controller works. It represents two regions, health checks, decision and safety stages, routing changes, event history and recovery. The application simulates these behaviours; it does not create real cloud regions or update live DNS records.

2. Objectives

Core learning objectives and system design goals

Show how an active region serves requests while a second region remains on standby.
Model health checks and a configurable consecutive-failure threshold.
Demonstrate quorum confirmation and safety checks before promotion.
Represent writer fencing to prevent both regions from accepting writes (Split Brain prevention).
Switch the simulated route after the standby is promoted and verified.
Handle unhealthy standby, high replication lag, quorum loss and routing failure gracefully.
Show controlled recovery and failback, with an immutable record of important system events.

4. Basic Concepts

Key architectural foundations of active-passive failover systems

4.1 Active-passive operation

The primary region receives normal traffic and is the only region allowed to accept writes. The standby region is prepared for recovery but does not serve normal write traffic until a safe promotion is completed.

4.2 Health checks and quorum

Health checks provide evidence about the condition of a service. Requiring several failed checks helps avoid a failover caused by one temporary delay. Quorum represents agreement among multiple monitors before the controller treats the primary failure as confirmed.

4.3 Fencing and split brain

Split brain occurs when two regions both believe they are primary and accept writes. Fencing represents the step that prevents the old primary from continuing to write before the standby is promoted. The simulation uses a state and fencing epoch to illustrate this rule; it is not a real distributed lease service.

4.4 Replication lag, RTO and RPO

Replication lag is the delay between a write being made in the primary and becoming available in the standby. High lag may mean that recent writes are missing, so the controller can block promotion. Recovery Time Objective (RTO) describes the target time to restore service, while Recovery Point Objective (RPO) describes the acceptable data-loss window.

5. System Architecture

Separation of client request path and resilient control plane

The architecture separates the request path from the control plane. Client requests pass through a traffic router to the active region. Health monitoring and failover logic operate separately: they evaluate both regions, check safety conditions, coordinate promotion and update the simulated route. The primary database replicates data to the standby database in the conceptual model.

6. System Flow

Operational flows during normal execution, automatic failover, and recovery

6.1 Normal Operation

Client → Traffic Router → Region A Application → Primary Database. The router sends normal traffic to Region A. Region B remains on standby, and the conceptual replication path keeps its database close to the primary state. Health checks monitor both regions and the event log records important state changes.

6.2 Automatic Failover (6-Step Sequence)

STEP 1 Suspected Failure Primary region begins failing health probes and is marked Suspected.
STEP 2 Quorum Check Failure threshold reached; monitor quorum confirms primary outage.
STEP 3 Safety & Lag Check Controller checks standby health and replication-lag policy limit.
STEP 4 Writer Fencing Old primary is fenced (lease revoked) to prevent stale writes.
STEP 5 Promotion & Route Region B is promoted; traffic router target is switched to Singapore.
STEP 6 Verification & Audit Verification probe checks route success; audit record logged.

6.3 Recovery and Failback

If Region A recovers after Region B has become active, the old primary must not automatically take control again. It remains in standby while its health and data state are checked. A controlled failback requires synchronization, safe writer ownership, an explicit action and route verification. This reduces the risk of two writable primaries or switching back to a region with outdated data.

9.2 Expected Learning Outcomes

  • A single health-check failure should not automatically trigger regional failover.
  • A safe failover needs a healthy standby and a decision that the primary has failed.
  • Writer fencing must happen before promoting the standby.
  • A routing change is not complete until the new route is verified.
  • Recovery of the old primary should be handled separately from failover.

10.1 Test Checklist

Normal start: Region A is active and Region B is standby.
Automatic failover: The route moves to B only after configured steps complete.
Safety gate failure: Quorum loss, unhealthy standby or high lag blocks promotion.
Recovery / failback: A recovered primary remains standby until explicitly switched back.

11. Observations

Key architectural takeaways from active-passive operations

  • Active-passive operation keeps normal traffic on one region and reserves the second for recovery.
  • The failover sequence contains several independent actions; detecting an outage does not by itself complete recovery.
  • The safety checks trade some recovery speed for a lower risk of split brain or promotion of an unready standby.
  • A route change must be followed by verification because updating the routing target alone does not prove that requests are succeeding.
  • Failback is a separate process. A recovered primary should first be synchronized and kept non-active until a controlled switch is approved.
12

12. Limitations

Simulation environment boundaries

  • The application simulates regional failures and routing decisions; it does not provision real geographic regions or cloud services.
  • The DNS / Route 53 component is a model. The application does not modify public DNS records or measure DNS cache propagation.
  • Database replication and lag are represented for demonstration. A real database replication topology is not deployed by the virtual lab.
  • Quorum, fencing and leases are demonstrated through simulation state and rules, not through a production consensus service.
13

13. Conclusion

Summary & engineering conclusions

The Multi-Region Active-Passive Failover Controller project demonstrates the main decisions needed to recover from a regional outage. FailoverLab brings together a two-region architecture, health monitoring, quorum checks, writer fencing, standby promotion, route switching, event logging and controlled failback in an interactive virtual lab.

The most important lesson is that safe failover is more than changing the active region. The controller must confirm the failure, make sure the standby is ready, prevent the old primary from writing, verify the new route and record the outcome.
