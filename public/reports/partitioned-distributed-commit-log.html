1. Abstract

Core executive summary & messaging design

This project studies a partitioned distributed commit log broker, a messaging design that allows applications to exchange events without depending on direct, synchronous calls between every service. Producers publish records to a topic, the topic is divided into partitions, and brokers store the records in an ordered log. Each partition has a leader replica and follower replicas to support copying and recovery. Consumer groups read records and track their progress using offsets. The virtual-lab model illustrates producers, a broker cluster, partitions, replication roles, a controller, and consumer groups. The project focuses on understanding partitioning, ordering, durability, consumer offsets, and fault tolerance. It is an educational design and prototype, not a production-ready broker.

02

2. Introduction

Context, decoupling services, and architectural motivation

Modern applications often contain separate services for orders, payments, carts, email, inventory, and analytics. If each service calls every other service directly, the system becomes tightly connected. A slow or unavailable service can affect the entire workflow. A distributed commit log provides an event-based communication layer: producers write events once, while different consumer groups can read those events independently and at their own pace.

The project uses a Kafka-style architecture as a learning reference. It demonstrates how a topic is split into partitions, how brokers store replicas, how a leader handles a partition, and how consumers poll records and commit offsets. The visuals show three producers, three brokers, three partitions, and two consumer groups. The design also includes a controller concept for metadata, heartbeats, and leader election.

03

3. Problem Definition

Decoupling and resilience goals

The main problem is that direct service-to-service communication can create tight coupling, reduce resilience, and make it difficult to replay missed events. When a receiving service is unavailable, events may be lost or the sender may fail. Traffic spikes can overload a slow receiver, and independent consumers may need separate integrations.

Core Design Goals

  • Decouple producers from consumers through a durable event log.
  • Divide a topic into partitions so work can be distributed across brokers.
  • Preserve record order within each partition.
  • Replicate partition data so a broker failure can be handled more safely.
  • Allow independent consumer groups to read and replay records using offsets.
  • Make system behaviour visible in a virtual lab for learning and evaluation.

5. Scope and Design Assumptions

In-scope components, out-of-scope items, and operational rules

5.1 In Scope

  • Topic named orders with 3 partitions (P0, P1, P2).
  • Three brokers, with one leader and follower copies for each partition.
  • Producer-side publishing from order, payment, and cart services.
  • Consumer groups for email and analytics workloads.
  • Polling and committing consumer offsets.
  • A conceptual controller for metadata, heartbeats, and leader election.

5.2 Out of Scope / Future

  • Production-grade authentication, authorization, and TLS.
  • Exactly-once transactions and advanced log compaction.
  • Cross-datacenter replication and production-grade multi-tenancy.
  • A fully resilient Raft controller unless separately implemented and tested.

5.3 Design Assumptions

The visual model is an educational simulation; visual elements do not by themselves prove that every broker protocol is implemented. The topic has three partitions and a replication factor of three, as represented in the architecture diagram. Records with keys are routed consistently to partitions using hash-based rules; records without keys use round-robin distribution. Leader replicas serve as primary read/write nodes, and synthetic test data is used throughout.

7. System Flow

Step-by-step produce, replicate, and consume execution flow

Step 1-3: Production & Partition Hashing

Producers create events (e.g. order-created records) and send them to the broker cluster. The partitioner hashes the record key to select a stable partition, and the leader replica appends the record to its ordered log.

Step 4-6: Replication & Consumption

Follower replicas fetch or copy the new record from the leader. The broker acknowledges the write according to the acknowledgement policy, and consumer group members poll the topic for records from assigned partitions.

Step 7-8: Offsets & Failover

After processing records, consumers commit offsets so they can resume from their saved position. If a leader fails, the controller checks eligible replicas and elects a new leader.

08

8. Theory and System Behaviour

Commit logs, partitioning, replication roles, and offset commits

8.1 Commit Log & Offsets

Append-only sequence of records. Each record has a monotonic offset identifying its position within a partition, allowing resumable reads.

8.2 Partitioning & Ordering

Ordering is strictly preserved within a single partition, not globally across all partitions. Keys ensure related events route to the same partition.

8.3 Replication & ISR

In-sync replicas copy logs from the leader. High watermarks define committed records safe for consumers to read.

10. Assessment and Evaluation

Verification criteria and test success metrics

Evaluation focuses on whether the model communicates the architecture correctly and whether the prototype behaves as expected under repeatable tests. Success criteria include: records are routed to valid partitions; order is preserved within a partition; consumers resume from committed offsets; UI telemetry metrics are captured honestly.

11. Sample Post-Test Questions

Architectural quiz and knowledge validation

Q. What is a partition? A. An ordered part of a topic that helps distribute data and processing.
Q. What is the role of a broker? A. It stores partitions and handles producer and consumer requests.
Q. What is the difference between a leader and a follower? A. The leader is the primary replica for a partition; followers copy its records.
Q. What is an offset? A. A record position within a partition.
Q. Why are consumer groups useful? A. They allow consumers to share work while different groups can read the same topic independently.

12. Feasibility and Limitations

Technical feasibility and prototype constraints

12.1 Feasibility

A conceptual prototype is feasible using common programming languages, file I/O, and local processes or containers with minimal cost and strong educational value.

12.2 Limitations

The simulation is conceptual and does not prove a complete commercial broker implementation. Custom prototypes may have bugs in replication, failover, or concurrency, and a single controller is a single point of failure.

14. Conclusion

Microproject wrap-up and engineering takeaway

The Partitioned Distributed Commit Log Broker project demonstrates the core ideas behind an event-based messaging system: producers publish records, partitions distribute the log, brokers store replicas, and consumer groups read data using offsets. The virtual lab provides a useful visual model of partitioned traffic and conceptual replication, while the logical architecture explains the role of leaders, followers, and the controller. The project is feasible as a learning-focused system design microproject. Its evaluation should be based on repeatable tests and recorded metrics, not assumptions.

15. References and Glossary

Documentation sources and domain terminology

Glossary
  • Commit log: An append-only ordered sequence of records.
  • Topic: A named stream of records.
  • Partition: An ordered slice of a topic and a unit of parallelism.
  • Offset: A record's position in a partition.
  • Broker: A server that stores partitions and handles requests.
  • Leader: The primary replica for a partition.
  • Follower: A replica that copies records from the leader.
  • Consumer group: Consumers that share partition work within the group.
